# W02 PIC 2.0 Operational Mapping and Benchmark Bridge

Date: 2026-06-23

## Purpose

This document maps each of the six PIC 2.0 model classes to its closest open-literature method family and to a concrete Week 3 benchmark item. Rather than matching each PIC class to a single public algorithm, it treats each class as an operational role in the robot decision stack and asks four questions: what decision does the module support, which public methods are closest, what failure mode remains under-studied, and how can that failure mode become a measurable benchmark scenario? Public robotics literature rarely provides an exact equivalent to an applied platform architecture, so a role-based mapping is more faithful than a one-to-one label match and more directly useful for benchmark design. Model-class names and roles follow InGen's Origami AI / PIC 2.0 platform documentation; this mapping restates only the architectural role of each class, not its implementation details.

## PIC 2.0 Role-Based Mapping

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimisation | Policy and action-selection layer for goal-directed robot behavior, including patrol or task choices, escalation decisions, and tradeoffs between reward, safety, and uncertainty. | Vision-language-action policies, robot foundation models, reinforcement learning from relative preferences, constrained or safety-aware policy optimization. Relevant public anchors include OpenVLA, Octo, pi0, Safe Robot Foundation Models, and safe RL literature. | GRPO should be evaluated as the layer that chooses among candidate actions under competing objectives. | STUM — Spatiotemporal Uncertainty Model | Uncertainty and spatio-temporal reliability layer that estimates whether the current perception or decision context is reliable enough for autonomous execution, review, or logging. | Conformal prediction, calibrated uncertainty, OOD detection, decision-theoretic abstention, and uncertainty-aware embodied evaluation. Relevant anchors include conformal decision theory and OOD safety assurance work. | STUM should be mapped to the decision gate between sensing and acting: it does not only estimate confidence, but also determines whether uncertainty should change the robot's operational mode. | Many public methods estimate uncertainty, but fewer connect uncertainty to concrete embodied decisions such as slowing down, requesting review, changing sensor reliance, or refusing an action. The gap is calibrated deferral in robotics-like scenarios. | Create uncertainty-gating scenarios with degraded observations, conflicting cues, temporal inconsistency, and missing context. | SEOM — Self-Supervised Ethical Oversight Mechanism | Constitutional safety layer that embeds domain-specific safety rules during policy training so the learned policy avoids unsafe actions by construction, rather than filtering them after the fact at runtime. | Safe RL, control barrier functions, constrained policy optimization, constitutional/rule-based training objectives, and (for contrast) runtime shields and safety filters for robot foundation models. Public anchors include Safe Robot Foundation Models, control barrier function surveys, and constraint-based safety methods. | SEOM should be understood as a training-time safety mechanism that shapes the policy through differentiable penalty signals, so unsafe actions are suppressed in the weights themselves. This contrasts with the runtime shields and post-hoc filters common in the open literature, which constrain an already-trained policy at inference. | Public safety methods are strongest when constraints are mathematically specified, but service robotics often involves mixed physical, social, and ethical constraints. The open gap is whether a model can apply such constraints without either ignoring risk or over-refusing benign tasks, and how training-time constitutional safety compares with runtime filtering. | Public open-weight baselines expose no training-time safety layer, so the benchmark tests the observable proxy. Build paired scenarios with and without safety-critical details, and score safety violation rate, correct blocking, correct permission, and whether the model identifies the relevant constraint rather than giving a generic safety answer. |
| AMDC — Adaptive Multi-Domain Calibration | Adaptive multi-domain calibration and sensor-fusion layer that decides how to weight or correct sensor streams when environmental conditions, drift, occlusion, or modality disagreement affect perception quality. | Multimodal sensor fusion, robust perception, calibration under distribution shift, sensor reliability estimation, and OOD-aware perception. | AMDC is upstream of policy selection but directly affects downstream decisions. It should be mapped as the layer that turns raw or conflicting observations into a usable state estimate and reliability signal. | Public foundation model evaluation often assumes a clean observation-text pair or a well-formed image input. The open gap is how degraded or conflicting sensor evidence should change the decision that follows, especially when one modality is plausible but unreliable. | Build sensor-conflict scenarios where visual, thermal, audio, map, or temporal cues disagree. Score whether the model identifies unreliable evidence, requests confirmation, or chooses a conservative action. |
| HTD-IRL — Hierarchical Task Decomposition via Inverse Reinforcement Learning | Hierarchical task decomposition and preference-learning layer for turning human goals, demonstrations, or workflows into subgoals, task sequences, and recovery behavior. | Hierarchical imitation learning, inverse reinforcement learning, learning from demonstrations, preference learning, task-and-motion planning, and embodied chain-of-thought style task decomposition. | HTD-IRL should be evaluated as the bridge between human intent and robot task structure. Its output is not a single action, but an ordered plan with subgoals, stopping criteria, and recovery steps. | Public embodied AI work increasingly studies generalist policies, but long-horizon service tasks still struggle with sparse demonstrations, ambiguous user preferences, interruptions, and recovery from failed subtasks. | Build long-horizon task scenarios with ambiguous instructions, missing information, user correction, or interrupted execution. Score subgoal quality, recovery behavior, user-alignment, and unnecessary escalation. |
| CRL-MRS — Cooperative Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Robot Systems | Fleet coordination layer that balances individual task goals with team-level cooperation for task allocation, patrol or coverage balancing, team response, communication constraints, and fleet-level optimization. | Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning, centralized-training/decentralized-execution methods, decentralized task allocation, graph-based coordination, resilient multi-robot systems, and multi-agent planning under communication limits. | CRL-MRS should be mapped as the team-level decision layer. It evaluates whether the fleet as a whole is achieving coverage, response, safety, and resource goals rather than whether one robot succeeds locally. | Public multi-agent methods often report simulation rewards, but applied service robot fleets need interpretable coordination under battery limits, communication delay, partial failure, and conflicting task priorities. | Build text or simulator-backed fleet scenarios with multiple robots, zones, incidents, and resource limits. Score allocation quality, robustness to robot failure, coordination explanation, and avoidance of duplicated or conflicting work. |

## How the Mapping Frames the Research Gaps

### Gap 1: Embodied models need calibrated uncertainty gates, not only task-success scores.

Public robot foundation model work shows that generalist policies can transfer across tasks and embodiments, but most evaluations still emphasize whether a task is completed. The missing piece is whether the model can identify when the input state is unreliable enough to require deferral, review, or conservative action. This gap connects primarily to STUM and AMDC: STUM supplies the uncertainty decision, while AMDC explains why multimodal evidence may become unreliable in the first place.

### Gap 2: Safety constraints need to be evaluated as decision behavior, not only described as principles.

Safe RL, control barrier functions, and safety-aware robot foundation model papers give useful mechanisms for constraining policies. However, public evaluations often do not test the mixed physical, social, and operational edge cases that service robots face. The mapping therefore frames safety as observable behavior: permit, block, defer, or modify an action under specific constraints. This gap connects primarily to SEOM and GRPO: SEOM shapes, at training time, which actions the policy will ever consider, while GRPO selects among the remaining actions under reward and constraint pressure.

### Gap 3: Human trust calibration requires explanations tied to robot uncertainty and limits.

HRI trust literature shows that overtrust and undertrust both matter. The open problem for service robots is not simply "make the robot explain itself." The explanation must help a user understand why the robot acted, what it was uncertain about, and when a human should intervene. This gap connects primarily to HTD-IRL, STUM, and CRL-MRS: HTD-IRL represents the human task goal, STUM represents the robot's uncertainty boundary, and CRL-MRS introduces fleet-level decisions that may be difficult for a user to interpret.

## Week 3 Benchmark Handoff

This mapping supports a Week 3 benchmark with three scenario groups:

| Scenario group | PIC classes emphasized | Example decision being tested | Candidate metrics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Uncertainty and sensor reliability | STUM, AMDC | Continue, defer, request more evidence, or choose a conservative action when observations are degraded or conflicting. | Correct deferral rate, unsafe overconfidence rate, false deferral rate, explanation specificity. |
| Safety-constrained action choice | SEOM, GRPO | Choose between task progress and constraint compliance under physical, social, or operational risk. | Safety violation rate, unnecessary refusal rate, task completion under constraints, constraint identification accuracy. |
| Human and fleet coordination | HTD-IRL, CRL-MRS, STUM | Decompose a human goal, recover from interruption, allocate robots, or explain a fleet-level decision. | Subgoal correctness, recovery quality, allocation quality, user-trust calibration, explanation usefulness. |

The Week 3 benchmark should therefore avoid generic "what should the robot do?" prompts. Each item should define the observation, goal, uncertainty source, constraint, available actions, expected safe behavior, and failure labels.
